# Run 2 batch 3: build in public and distribution

This batch examines the four selected `#BuildInPublic` winners. The controls
are not presumed to have entered the same category because Devpost does not
reliably expose a non-winner's category selections. They are matched on year,
platform, product shape, and the public evidence they did provide. Scores are
the analyst's interpretation of the historical criteria, not judges' scores.

## Meshing

**Winner and match.** Meshing won the 2024 `#BuildInPublic` award. One Step is
the control: both are 2024-era, solo-built SwiftUI productivity or creation
tools that used RevenueCat and were moved from procrastination or prototype to
submission by Ship-a-ton. One Step's page date and event wording make the year
match likely, but its missing event backlink lowers control confidence to
medium.

### 1. Track interpretation

The 2024 wording rewarded an interesting public development journey,
interesting lessons, and ideas introduced through community feedback. Meshing
interpreted this as a continuous feedback system: regular X posts, a daily
YouTube log, visible struggles, and named product changes. The organizer tied
noise, animation, text masking, favorites, and clipboard export directly to
community feedback. Meshing therefore solved the track narrowly rather than
using audience size as a proxy.

One Step described only the deadline's motivational effect and asked for ASO
tips. No public chronology, response, or feedback-to-change chain is visible.

### 2. Product thesis

Meshing targets developers and non-designers who want a SwiftUI mesh gradient
without waiting for slow Xcode previews or mastering a professional design
tool. The triggering situation is an idea, photo, or need for a background;
the workaround is code-and-preview iteration or a general design tool. Its job
is “quickly create a beautiful gradient,” and its wedge is live, direct mesh
manipulation plus image and code export. The founder insight is observed in the
Starbucks/Xcode-preview story.

One Step targets people who use only the basic features of several productivity
apps. Its promise is consolidation: activity logging, statistics, lists, and
widgets. The stated pain is real, but the segment and highest-priority job are
broad; even the builder recognized the risk of feature proliferation.

### 3. Core loop

Meshing's loop is: prompt, photo, or template trigger → manipulate control
points or colors → see the mesh change immediately → refine, animate, mask, or
add texture → export image or code → reuse or share → return for another asset.
The visible value moment occurs before purchase. Favorites build a lightweight
library; shared outputs can expose the product.

One Step's loop is log an activity → accumulate entries → see time statistics
or a widget → log again. Lists introduce a second loop. The value is plausible,
but the submission does not show which loop controls retention or the paid
moment.

### 4. Journey

Meshing can start with an editable template, reducing blank-canvas friction.
Creation is followed by editing and export; RevenueCat gates paid value, though
the event-period package and paywall timing are not visible. The App Store
approval is described. VoiceOver and Voice Control were added late. Loading,
purchase recovery, cancellation, and export-error paths remain unknown.

One Step exposes a feed, logging dashboard, statistics, lists, and widgets.
Firebase authentication and database use imply an account or sync path, but
onboarding, permission timing, first-value duration, paywall, purchase restore,
and recovery are not documented.

### 5. UI and interaction

Meshing's event image centers the generated canvas, then presents six large
actions—Craft, Arrange, Refine, Animate, Mask, and Save. Predicted attention:
first fixation on the gradient, scan to the branded title, then to the two-row
action grid. The large lower controls are thumb-reachable; the top heart and
settings controls are secondary. Direct tap and rotate gestures fit the object
being edited, but their discoverability and undo behavior are not visible.

One Step's dark feed groups entries by date and uses emoji filters and a
floating four-item tab bar. Predicted attention starts on the newest log entry,
then the category row, then the tab bar. The feed is readable but the large
serif type, emoji taxonomy, activity units, and multiple modules create more
interpretation work than Meshing's single canvas.

### 6. Visual system

Meshing uses the output itself as color system and hero image. White interface
chrome, bold black labels, outlined tiles, and saturated gradient borders keep
the generated art dominant. The wordmark is distinctive but visually busy.
The screenshots communicate creative range better than a generic feature list.

One Step uses black, charcoal cards, white serif text, gray metadata, and emoji
icons. Contrast is generally strong in the inspected feed. The typeface gives
personality but weakens numerical scanning, and the unrelated emoji styles make
the system less cohesive.

### 7. UX quality

Meshing's time to visual value appears short, and the template start contains
scope. The builder reported performance work on animated meshes and a late
accessibility pass. Privacy treatment for AI prompts and imported photos,
offline behavior, destructive-edit confirmation, and failure states remain
unknown. There is no visible dark-pattern evidence.

One Step's manual activity logging imposes recurring effort before statistics
become useful. It may reward a committed user, but no empty-state coaching or
restraint strategy is shown. Firebase data handling, deletion, export, and
accessibility are unknown.

### 8. Monetization

Meshing used RevenueCat, but contemporaneous price, trial, free limits, and
paid boundary are unavailable. Exportable assets and code are credible paid
value because payment can follow a completed creation. One Step also used
RevenueCat, but disclosed no packaging or reason a user would pay rather than
use separate established apps.

### 9. Growth and retention

Meshing's public loop did three jobs: accountability, product discovery, and
feature research. Everyday visual references prompted messages; the builder
then prioritized requests and published component write-ups. Reusable outputs
and a proposed MeshRoom could support sharing, but no acquisition, retention,
or revenue metric is available.

One Step disclosed no launch, audience, experiment, or retention result. The
builder explicitly identified uncertain ASO and “Marketing” as future work.

### 10. Technical and operational shape

Meshing used SwiftUI, Core Graphics, Core Image, Metal, AIProxy, WishKit, and
RevenueCat. Real-time and animated mesh rendering created a disclosed
performance risk, especially on smaller devices. AI generation adds inference
cost and network dependence; local manual editing and export may remain useful
without it. A social MeshRoom was correctly deferred because it would add a
backend, moderation, and network burden.

One Step used SwiftUI and Firebase Analytics, Authentication, and Database.
That is a smaller stack, but combining tracking, lists, widgets, and proposed
app blocking creates several state, sync, and Screen Time seams. No release or
test evidence is provided.

### 11. Submission craft

Meshing's story has a memorable inciting incident, visible emotional lows,
technical constraints, named community requests, an App Store endpoint, and a
demo centered on the transformation. The screenshot sequence covers AI,
templates, wireframe, grain, export, and the icon. The proof is observable even
without audience metrics.

One Step's Devpost page is candid but inventory-like. It shows five product
surfaces and acknowledges positioning risk, yet it lacks a demo, public-work
timeline, feedback receipts, store link, and proof that feedback altered the
app.

### What the evidence supports

Meshing supplies a traceable public-input → prioritization → shipped-change
chain. One Step shows that merely crediting a deadline or asking for tips is not
equivalent evidence.

### What remains unknown

The archive does not quantify Meshing's post cadence, conversion, retention,
or individual change dates. One Step's exact category entry and store status
cannot be confirmed.

### What may transfer to 2026

A judge-readable change ledger can make public work legible: the user request,
decision, trade-off, shipped artifact, and observed result. Post volume alone
should not replace that chain.

## Gurwi – Learn Anything

**Winner and match.** Gurwi won first place in 2025 `#BuildInPublic`. Memory
Hammer is the control: both are 2025 Android-first learning products, both
converted a long incubation into a Shipaton release, both used public feedback,
and both reported material adoption. This is a strong product and platform
match, although public category-entry status is unknown.

### 1. Track interpretation

The 2025 criteria were Sharing Your Story, Engagement, and Lessons Learned;
audience size explicitly did not matter. Gurwi nevertheless provided both a
deep founder narrative and large reach. Its public story connected education
exclusion, a 2024 idea-validation video, more than a year of development, the
Shipaton release, twelve updates, product experiments, and dated results. It
reframed build-in-public from a hackathon diary into a continuing mission and
market-validation record.

Memory Hammer described learning to share on camera and cycling build,
promotion, and feedback, but the submission preserves less of the individual
posts and specific decisions caused by them.

### 2. Product thesis

Gurwi prioritizes Colombian and Latin American learners underserved by formal
education, then broadens to anyone who wants practical knowledge in short,
visual, interactive, multilingual lessons. The trigger is a knowledge gap; the
workarounds are long video, PDFs, and fragmented research. The wedge is the
`.gurwi` lesson format plus its editor. Founder insight is unusually specific:
Camilo's own transition from school to adult systems and a viral 2024 proposal.

Memory Hammer serves students, professionals, and people worried about memory.
It replaces manual flashcard authoring with AI extraction from text, photos, or
visual mnemonics, then uses FSRS review. The automation wedge is clear, but the
segment is broader and some age-related messaging raises trust concerns.

### 3. Core loop

Gurwi: choose a class → advance through a short page sequence → read, listen,
interact, or answer → complete the lesson → gain points/streaks/rank → return
for another class. The value moment is a complex idea made understandable.
Subscription exposure occurred as early as post-registration in an experiment.

Memory Hammer: capture or upload material → generate cards or a mnemonic →
review with FSRS → receive scheduled reminders → preserve recall → add more
material. The habit loop is stronger than its public-story loop and closely
aligned with notification timing.

### 4. Journey

Gurwi's inspected welcome screen leads with illustrations, the claim that
learning need not be boring, Get Started, and Log In. Lessons support three
languages, playable narration, questions, source access, streaks, and ranking.
Only five classes were available at the event snapshot, limiting repeat
breadth. Account deletion, offline learning, purchase recovery, and content
error recovery are not visible.

Memory Hammer's journey moves from motivation-based onboarding and notification
permission to input capture, generated card, review, free-trial limits, and a
theme-adaptive RevenueCat paywall. Upgrade is accessible from a limits widget
and account page. Its documented re-engagement journeys can continue across
push and email; the tone can become coercive.

### 5. UI and interaction

Gurwi's first screen uses a saturated yellow field, four illustrated learner
portraits, a black Get Started button, and a secondary login link. Predicted
attention: logo → portraits → “Learning doesn't have to be boring” → primary
button. The lesson demo shows a single Continue action unlocking one visual or
interactive page at a time, which restrains decision density. Playback controls
reuse a media-player model.

Memory Hammer's inspected asset uses an Android-native camera composition: the
source fills the view, close and flash controls sit at the top, and the large
shutter occupies the thumb zone. Its promotional layout explains the feature,
but later card, review, and messaging screens introduce a larger system than
the first action suggests.

### 6. Visual system

Gurwi uses bold yellow, black, white-framed illustration cards, rounded
controls, and an intentionally playful display face. Color blocks and custom
art distinguish it from generic course catalogs. The line length and large
type on lesson pages are not fully inspectable.

Memory Hammer uses a purple promotional field and mostly standard camera and
Material patterns. The hammer mascot adds identity across messages. The many
communication templates are consistent in concept, but no systematic contrast
or accessibility evidence is supplied.

### 7. UX quality

Gurwi's page-at-a-time lesson model can lower cognitive load and offers visual,
auditory, and multilingual routes. Source access supports trust. However,
educational accuracy, editorial review, accessibility, and safety across broad
topics are not independently verified. A subscription prompt immediately
after registration increased subscriptions by builder report, but it may also
interrupt first value.

Memory Hammer reduces card-authoring labor and supports offline local review
through its flashcard model, though server and sync behavior are not fully
specified. “You're going to be dumb” and “You're going to forget everything”
re-engagement copy risks fear and shame, particularly for vulnerable users.

### 8. Monetization

Gurwi combines limited content with subscription access. The team reports that
moving the prompt to immediately after registration increased subscriptions,
but gives no controlled-test size or conversion rate. The October 3 update
reports 228 active subscriptions and $1,145 MRR. The content-studio cost makes
paid access logical but operationally demanding.

Memory Hammer offered a seven-day trial covering three creation modes with
generation limits, then premium. Payment follows demonstrated card generation,
and RevenueCat/OneSignal tags coordinate entitlement-aware messaging. Exact
price and realized subscription count are absent.

### 9. Growth and retention

Gurwi's original idea video reportedly reached 200,000 views and 40,000 likes.
During the reported Shipaton window the team published twelve releases. The
October 3 snapshot reports 17,696 users, 3.3 million social views, 249,600
likes, and acquisition dominated by TikTok and Instagram. These are builder-
reported and later than the judging deadline, so they cannot all be treated as
judge-visible simultaneously.

Memory Hammer reports 3,000 installs and 2,000 users within three weeks. It
segments by motivation and behavior, then uses in-app, push, and email journeys.
This is deeper lifecycle instrumentation than Gurwi publicly describes, which
means notification sophistication is not a safe winner differentiator.

### 10. Technical and operational shape

Gurwi uses Flutter, Supabase/PostgreSQL, Firebase/Google Cloud, a Next.js
editor, and a custom format. Its disclosed 10 schemas, 65 tables, 39 functions,
36 triggers, and 3,000-line lesson files create enormous solo/small-team
operational risk. Content production, translation, fact review, and format
compatibility are recurring costs.

Memory Hammer uses Flutter, an AI generation service, FSRS, sync, RevenueCat,
and OneSignal. Android-only focus reduced simultaneous release risk. Inference,
image processing, and aggressive multi-channel messaging create cost, privacy,
and fatigue risks.

### 11. Submission craft

Gurwi's demo moves from problem and founder to the lesson experience, the
editor, sources, reach, ratings, revenue, and twelve updates. It makes the
consumer surface and production infrastructure judge-visible. The Devpost
story is long but supplies a clear causal spine.

Memory Hammer's submission is exceptionally detailed about lifecycle tooling,
shows many screenshots, and reports adoption. Its public-story evidence is less
organized around specific public comment → decision → result examples, while
its message-template detail can obscure the core learning value.

### 12. Rubric mapping

| Historical criterion | Gurwi evidence | Counterevidence | Analyst score | Control score | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---:|---:|---|
| Sharing the story | Founder narrative, dated launch/update arc, dedicated campaign page, posts and video | Some foundational validation predates the official period | 5 | 4 | High/medium |
| Engagement | Large reported views, likes, reviews, store rankings, and feedback | Metrics are self-reported; date snapshots differ | 5 | 4 | Medium |
| Lessons learned | Questions, paywall timing, content tooling, and release strategy changed through testing | Causal experiment details are incomplete | 5 | 3 | Medium |

### What the evidence supports

Gurwi paired public narrative with real product, distribution, and experiment
evidence. Memory Hammer demonstrates that large adoption and sophisticated
retention automation also existed outside the winners.

### What remains unknown

Neither project's public category selection, audited metrics, retention, paid
conversion, or educational outcomes are available. Gurwi's five-class catalog
and Memory Hammer's safety claims were not independently evaluated.

### What may transfer to 2026

Keep dated metric snapshots separate and connect public feedback to a decision
that judges can see in a released build. A large reach figure strengthens, but
does not substitute for, the required learning chain.

## Echo Reminder

**Winner and match.** Echo Reminder won second place in 2025
`#BuildInPublic`. Stupido is the control: both are iOS-oriented, voice-first
task products created around the friction of maintaining reminders, both were
AI-assisted first launches, and both shipped event-period updates from user
feedback. Echo used React Native/Expo; Stupido used SwiftUI.

### 1. Track interpretation

Echo documented daily on social media, including disagreements, beginner
mistakes, audience-selection uncertainty, parsing trade-offs, and the eventual
launch. It used feedback both to narrow its audience and to defer a calendar
view. The evidence meets all three criteria, even though exact post reach is
not reported.

Stupido tells a candid non-technical-founder story and names feedback it
accepted or rejected. Its Devpost account is strong, but a preserved daily
public archive or proof that the community discussion itself inspired others
is not visible.

### 2. Product thesis

Echo prioritizes people in their early twenties who are moving, cognitively
loaded, and need to capture a thought before acting. The workaround is tapping
through Apple Reminders or unreliable Siri parsing. Its wedge is converting one
utterance—including multiple, recurring, or self-corrected tasks—into reviewed,
structured reminders, with optional future research.

Stupido begins with the founder's wife saying she had no time to maintain a
to-do list. Its wedge is also “press a big red button and yap,” with automatic
categorization and voice updates. The similarity makes this a strong control:
voice-first simplicity is not unique to the winner.

### 3. Core loop

Echo: thought occurs → record one utterance → inspect parsed tasks, dates,
notes, repeats, and checklists → save → receive notification or AI research at
the due time → complete or repeat. Smart AI introduces a premium moment after
the base capture demonstrates value.

Stupido: dump tasks by voice → receive a sorted list → swipe status, drag order,
or update by voice → complete → return when overloaded. Scheduling and native
Reminders integration were added before the deadline.

### 4. Journey

Echo's demo opens directly on a Today/Upcoming list with a large record control.
It demonstrates three difficult cases, review before save, smart-AI trial
unlock, RevenueCat paywall, and three widget shapes. Local notifications and a
small offline cache preserve essential reminders. Permission timing,
transcription failure, cancellation, deletion, and restore are not shown.

Stupido's first action is also a large red record button. Voice generates
categorized tasks; users swipe and drag rather than navigate menus. App Store,
subscription, entitlement, and update work are described, but first-launch,
permissions, paywall, errors, and recovery are not preserved.

### 5. UI and interaction

Echo's demo frame is extremely restrained: settings and add at the top,
Completed and Overdue summaries, Today/Upcoming sections, and a large bottom
record button. Predicted attention: red recording control → Today list →
overdue indicator → add/settings. The primary action is thumb-reachable; status
text is visually quieter. Complex parses are reviewed in conventional form
fields, which protects against silent automation errors.

Stupido's promotional frame uses oversized black display type, red progress
labels, rounded status rows, a celebratory gradient, and a robot mascot.
Predicted attention: promise → red task/status path → character. The design is
more expressive than Echo's, but the actual in-app information architecture is
less completely evidenced.

### 6. Visual system

Echo uses white, black, purple action accents, green completion, and red
overdue/recording states. Hierarchy is familiar and functional. Its split-screen
demo keeps product and builder visible, supporting authenticity at the expense
of some UI scale.

Stupido uses a bold rounded display font, saturated red, lime/cyan celebration,
and a custom crowned robot. It is more brand-distinctive, showing that visual
personality alone did not determine the public-process award.

### 7. UX quality

Echo keeps the user in control by presenting parsed details before save and
uses schemas, repair logic, and deduplication. Latency and nondeterminism remain
disclosed risks. Audio, task content, web search, and AI summaries create
privacy boundaries; deletion and data-retention policies are unknown. A
calendar was deferred until audience validation, reducing premature density.

Stupido protects simplicity by rejecting complex workflows and adding only
compatible requests. The App Store launch proves a working end-to-end path.
Voice privacy, transcription recovery, accessibility, and task-loss handling
are unknown.

### 8. Monetization

Echo gates smart research/web search behind a RevenueCat trial while basic
structured capture remains the product wedge. This aligns payment to compute-
heavy enrichment, though the demo does not state price or limits. Stupido
reports about $300 from paid users and uses RevenueCat/Superwall, but the paid
package and trial narrative are not disclosed.

### 9. Growth and retention

Echo's daily vlog supplied accountability and qualitative feedback; the team
plans TikTok UGC and Reddit because development displaced marketing. Widgets,
scheduled reminders, and eventual calendar integration support return use, but
no user, retention, or revenue metrics are disclosed.

Stupido reports more than 1,000 downloads in its first weeks, roughly $300,
positive feedback, and a version 0.2.0 with scheduling, reminders, and native
Reminders. It has stronger disclosed market numbers than Echo, controlling
against the idea that traction alone explains placement.

### 10. Technical and operational shape

Echo uses Expo/React Native, TypeScript, local notification and storage APIs,
Supabase, an Express/Railway upload endpoint, Whisper, OpenAI Responses, and
Perplexity. One endpoint reduces touchpoints, but AI/audio vendors, latency,
schema drift, and recurring web-search cost remain. An incomplete LLM-judged
eval system is described.

Stupido uses SwiftUI, CloudKit, multiple AI/inference vendors, OneSignal,
RevenueCat, and Superwall. Native integration may improve platform fit, but the
vendor count increases operational seams. The builder's non-technical status
raises maintenance risk, which was mitigated through narrow interactions.

### 11. Submission craft

Echo's demo proves three complex parses, repeat rules, details, paywall, AI
research, and widgets in a compact arc. The written story exposes disagreement,
mistakes, test limitations, and roadmap deferrals. Judges can see both the
mechanism and what the team learned publicly.

Stupido's story is memorable and its reported outcomes are strong. The asset
sequence explains status transitions and brand, but the preserved evidence
does not make its public posting cadence or individual community conversations
as judge-observable.

### 12. Rubric mapping

| Historical criterion | Echo evidence | Counterevidence | Analyst score | Control score | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---:|---:|---|
| Sharing the story | Daily vlog, visible partnership and candid process narrative | Post archive and cadence count unavailable | 5 | 4 | Medium |
| Engagement | Feedback exposed audience ambiguity and small product details | Reach and response metrics absent | 4 | 3 | Medium |
| Lessons learned | Audience narrowed; calendar deferred; parsing and feedback trade-offs documented | Exact public comment-to-change receipts absent | 5 | 4 | High/medium |

### What the evidence supports

Echo made public uncertainty, decision-making, and a working complex flow easy
to inspect. Stupido matches much of the product thesis and exceeds Echo on
reported downloads, revenue, and visible brand craft.

### What remains unknown

Public reach, retention, conversion, error rates, and category selection remain
unknown. Neither project discloses a rigorous privacy assessment for voice and
task content.

### What may transfer to 2026

Showing what feedback was rejected can be as credible as showing what was
accepted when the decision protects a coherent audience and loop.

## Tomo Japan

**Winner and match.** Tomo Japan won third place in 2025 `#BuildInPublic`.
Rangelert is the control: both are solo SwiftUI travel utilities built in 2025,
use Firebase, RevenueCat, and public feedback, and depend on location or travel
contexts. Tomo documents a much richer evidence trail.

### 1. Track interpretation

Tomo used separate channels for technical progress, personal learning, and
travel-content validation, then published Medium retrospectives. Public input
changed content priorities, architecture, bugs, paywall performance, and
accessibility. It interprets the track as public product development plus a
career-change narrative.

Rangelert states that its Discord and social sharing gathered feedback and
tips, but preserves no dated story, quoted response, or named app change. It is
a useful minimum-evidence comparison, not a weak product judgment.

### 2. Product thesis

Tomo prioritizes Japan travelers overwhelmed by scattered guides, social
posts, links, and personal research. It combines curated destinations, hidden
gems, seasonal recommendations, itineraries, tips, restaurants, and transport
links. Its wedge is a personal, curated pocket friend grounded in the founder's
Japan experience and long-running goal to ship this exact app.

Rangelert targets train travelers afraid of missing a stop or needing time to
prepare. Its wedge is a radius-before-destination alarm with notifications,
sound, and haptics. The job is narrower and clear, but less public research is
shown.

### 3. Core loop

Tomo: choose a destination → scan curated context → open things to do, food,
seasonal advice, or itinerary → follow map/transport links during travel →
return for another place; lifetime Pro unlocks more destinations. Sharing is
content-led rather than a built-in UGC loop.

Rangelert: choose destination and radius → travel → receive advance alarm →
prepare or exit → configure another trip. Utility repeats per journey; IAP and
promo offers are disclosed but not tied to a visible moment.

### 4. Journey

Tomo's welcome screen offers Sign In and Continue as Guest, then Discover,
city, seasonal, itinerary, and location views. Guest access reduces account
friction. Links hand off directions and restaurants to Google Maps. Version
1.1 improved a slow paywall, UI polish, and accessibility after user videos.
Offline content, link failure, content freshness, restore, and cancellation are
unknown.

Rangelert's actual setup path is not visible beyond banner assets. MapKit,
radius units, notifications, sound, and haptics imply location and notification
permissions whose timing is critical. Background execution and missed-alert
recovery are not documented.

### 5. UI and interaction

Tomo's welcome image uses a full-bleed destination photo, translucent white
copy card, Mount Fuji mascot, blue Sign In, and lighter Guest button. Predicted
attention: mascot/copy card → vivid Sign In → guest option → photo. The primary
button is reachable; the account-first emphasis may momentarily outrank the
lower-friction guest path. The demo shows conventional tab and vertical content
navigation rather than novel gestures.

Rangelert's inspected asset is a brand splash with a map-pin/route icon and
soft pastel blur. It proves branding, not the core alarm flow. No defensible
attention, thumb reach, or decision-density analysis of the working UI is
possible.

### 6. Visual system

Tomo combines documentary travel photography, dark charcoal framing, rounded
translucent panels, blue actions, Japanese script, and a kawaii Mount Fuji
mascot. The brand is memorable and relevant, though dense guide pages were
reported as text-heavy and scheduled for more imagery.

Rangelert's green/blue/yellow icon clearly signals map and radius. The blurred
cream/pastel background is calm, but its outlined italic wordmark is less
legible and no in-app system is shown.

### 7. UX quality

Tomo cut map view, favorites, and in-app itinerary creation to ship. The guest
path and curated itineraries shorten first value. Version 1.1 provides a rare
recovery example: three users sent videos of a slow paywall and the builder
fixed it. Authentication, analytics, remote media, location links, and travel
advice require privacy and freshness discipline not fully documented.

Rangelert's safety depends on permission clarity, reliable background location,
alarm delivery, units, battery impact, and a fallback when an alert fails.
Inclusivity and haptics are described, but these paths are not evidenced.

### 8. Monetization

Tomo sells Lifetime Pro as a one-time unlock for all destination content after
a free core selection. This suits a travel utility and avoids asking users to
maintain a subscription between trips. The event price is unavailable. The
initial paywall performance failure shows that technically correct
monetization can still harm trust.

Rangelert uses RevenueCat IAP and OneSignal promotional offers, but price,
package, trial, paywall placement, and the paid value boundary are unknown.

### 9. Growth and retention

Tomo used X for technical building, TikTok for personal and content experiments,
Medium for long-form learning, and Instagram surveys for destination priority.
The submission reports 32,000 X views, 780 engagements, and 65 followers in 60
days, plus 26,000 TikTok views, 1,100 likes, and 115 followers in 30 days.
These are builder-reported, not audited. Travel seasonality and catalog breadth
will govern retention.

Rangelert mentions Discord and social channels but supplies no metrics,
experiment record, store response, or behavioral retention mechanism beyond
repeat travel.

### 10. Technical and operational shape

Tomo uses SwiftUI/MVVM, Firebase Authentication/Firestore/Storage/Functions,
Supabase for SQL validation, JavaScript ETL, Google Docs authoring, Gemini, and
RevenueCat. The custom SQL-to-JSON-to-Firestore pipeline improved content work
after public advice, but it adds many solo-maintainer seams. Image processing,
content accuracy, broken external links, and destination expansion are ongoing
costs.

Rangelert uses SwiftUI, SwiftData, iCloud, MapKit, Firebase, OneSignal, and
RevenueCat. This is a large stack for one alarm loop. Background location,
notification reliability, geofence limits, and macOS expansion are release
risks.

### 11. Submission craft

Tomo's demo first shows the destination, itinerary, map-link, and purchase
surfaces, then explains founder, architecture, community decisions, launch,
feedback, and requested features. Five ordered screenshots support the journey.
The written submission names a specific paywall bug and fix, making iteration
judge-observable.

Rangelert's three banner images and short copy establish the concept but do not
prove permission onboarding, map setup, alert firing, or feedback-driven
change. Its statement of public sharing is not a substitute for linked receipts.

### 12. Rubric mapping

| Historical criterion | Tomo evidence | Counterevidence | Analyst score | Control score | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---:|---:|---|
| Sharing the story | Daily X work, TikTok, Medium trilogy, launch and personal arc | Full post archive not captured | 5 | 3 | High/medium |
| Engagement | Reported views/engagement; surveys, advice, user bug videos | Small accounts and self-reported totals | 4 | 2 | Medium |
| Lessons learned | Content, architecture, scope, paywall, UI, and accessibility changes | Only one week of post-launch iteration | 5 | 2 | High/low |

### What the evidence supports

Tomo shows that public work can reduce technical and market uncertainty before
and after launch. Rangelert shows a similarly relevant native product whose
public-sharing claim lacks an inspectable decision trail.
